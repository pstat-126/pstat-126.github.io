Review: OLS Estimators

From last time, the OLS estimators are:

\[\widehat{\beta}_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}, \qquad \widehat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \widehat{\beta}_1\bar{x}\]

They are unbiased, linear, and the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (Gauss-Markov).

Today: What do the residuals tell us, and how do we estimate the unknown \(\sigma^2\)?

Learning Objectives

By the end of today you will be able to:

  • Define residuals and explain what they measure
  • State and prove the four properties of OLS residuals
  • Define RSS and explain its role in regression
  • Compute and interpret \(\widehat{\sigma}^2\) and \(\widehat{\sigma}\)

Residuals

What are Residuals?

After fitting the model, the residual for observation \(i\) is:

\[e_i = y_i - \widehat{y}_i = y_i - \widehat{\beta}_0 - \widehat{\beta}_1 x_i\]

Residuals measure how far each observation falls from the fitted line:

  • \(e_i > 0\): observed value is above the fitted line
  • \(e_i < 0\): observed value is below the fitted line
  • \(e_i = 0\): observation falls exactly on the fitted line

Note

Residuals \(e_i\) are the observable counterparts to the unobservable errors \(\varepsilon_i\). We use them to check model assumptions and estimate \(\sigma^2\).

Properties of OLS Residuals

  1. \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n e_i = 0\) — residuals sum to zero
  2. \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n \widehat{y}_i = \sum_{i=1}^n y_i\) — sum of fitted values equals sum of observed values
  3. \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n x_i e_i = 0\) — residuals are uncorrelated with the predictor
  4. \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n \widehat{y}_i e_i = 0\) — residuals are uncorrelated with the fitted values
  5. The fitted line always passes through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\)

Estimating \(\sigma^2\)

Residual Sum of Squares

The Residual Sum of Squares (RSS) measures the total unexplained variation:

\[\text{RSS} = \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \widehat{y}_i)^2\]

  • A smaller RSS means the line fits the data more closely
  • RSS = 0 only if every point lies exactly on the fitted line

Estimating \(\sigma^2\)

The error variance \(\sigma^2 = \text{Var}(\varepsilon_i)\) is unknown. We estimate it using the residuals.

A natural starting point is the average squared residual, but we must divide by \(n - 2\), not \(n\):

\[\widehat{\sigma}^2 = \text{MSE} = \frac{\text{RSS}}{n - 2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2}{n-2}\]

This quantity is also called the Mean Square Error (MSE).

Why \(n - 2\)? Estimating \(\widehat{\beta}_0\) and \(\widehat{\beta}_1\) uses 2 degrees of freedom since two parameters are being used.

It can be shown that \(\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\sigma}^2) = \sigma^2\), so \(\widehat{\sigma}^2\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\).

Proof: Steps 1 & 2

Goal: Show \(\mathbb{E}(\text{MSE}) = \sigma^2\), i.e., \(\mathbb{E}(\text{RSS}) = (n-2)\sigma^2\).

Step 1 — rewrite RSS. Substitute \(\widehat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \widehat{\beta}_1\bar{x}\) to center the residuals:

Step 2 — expand the square. Use \(\sum(y_i-\bar{y})(x_i-\bar{x}) = \widehat{\beta}_1 S_{xx}\), then take expectations:

Proof: Step 3 — Piece 1

Evaluate Piece 1: \(\mathbb{E}\!\left(\sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2\right)\)

Substitute \(y_i - \bar{y} = \beta_1(x_i - \bar{x}) + (\varepsilon_i - \bar{\varepsilon})\) and expand the square:

Recall: Model Assumption

\(\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_i) = 0\) is a model assumption (errors are mean-zero). Then by linearity of expectation: \(\mathbb{E}(\bar{\varepsilon}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_i) = 0\).

Proof: Step 3 — Piece 2

Evaluate Piece 2: \(\mathbb{E}\!\left(\widehat{\beta}_1^2\, S_{xx}\right)\)

Since \(S_{xx}\) is non-random, this reduces to finding \(\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\beta}_1^2)\).

Use the identity: \(\mathbb{E}(Z^2) = \text{Var}(Z) + \left[\mathbb{E}(Z)\right]^2\)

Proof: Step 4 — Combine

We now have both pieces. Plug them into \(\mathbb{E}(\text{RSS}) = \text{Piece 1} - \text{Piece 2}\):

\(\mathbb{E}(\text{MSE}) = \mathbb{E}\!\left(\dfrac{\text{RSS}}{n-2}\right) =\)

Proof: Steps 1 & 2 (Solution)

Step 1 — rewrite RSS. Substitute \(\widehat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \widehat{\beta}_1\bar{x}\):

\[\text{RSS} = \sum(y_i - \widehat{y}_i)^2 = \sum\!\left[(y_i - \bar{y}) - \widehat{\beta}_1(x_i - \bar{x})\right]^2\]

Step 2 — expand the square. Since \(\sum(y_i-\bar{y})(x_i-\bar{x}) = \widehat{\beta}_1 S_{xx}\):

\[= \sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2 - 2\widehat{\beta}_1^2 S_{xx} + \widehat{\beta}_1^2 S_{xx} = \sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2 - \widehat{\beta}_1^2 S_{xx}\]

Proof: Step 3 — Piece 1 (Solution)

Evaluate Piece 1: \(\mathbb{E}\!\left(\sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2\right)\)

Substitute \(y_i - \bar{y} = \beta_1(x_i-\bar{x}) + (\varepsilon_i - \bar{\varepsilon})\) and expand:

\[\sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2 = \beta_1^2 S_{xx} + 2\beta_1\!\sum(x_i-\bar{x})(\varepsilon_i-\bar{\varepsilon}) + \sum(\varepsilon_i-\bar{\varepsilon})^2\]

Taking expectations — the cross term vanishes since \(\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_i - \bar\varepsilon) = 0\):

\[\mathbb{E}\!\left(\sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2\right) = \beta_1^2 S_{xx} + (n-1)\sigma^2\]

Proof: Step 3 — Piece 2 (Solution)

Evaluate Piece 2: \(\mathbb{E}\!\left(\widehat{\beta}_1^2\, S_{xx}\right)\)

Since \(S_{xx}\) is non-random: \(\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\beta}_1^2 S_{xx}) = S_{xx} \cdot \mathbb{E}(\widehat{\beta}_1^2)\).

Apply \(\mathbb{E}(Z^2) = \text{Var}(Z) + [\mathbb{E}(Z)]^2\) with \(Z = \widehat{\beta}_1\):

\[\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\beta}_1^2) = \text{Var}(\widehat{\beta}_1) + \left[\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\beta}_1)\right]^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}} + \beta_1^2\]

Therefore: \(\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\beta}_1^2 S_{xx}) = \sigma^2 + \beta_1^2 S_{xx}\)

Proof: Step 4 — Combine (Solution)

Now plug both pieces in:

\[\mathbb{E}(\text{RSS}) = \Big[\beta_1^2 S_{xx} + (n-1)\sigma^2\Big] - \Big[\sigma^2 + \beta_1^2 S_{xx}\Big]\]

The \(\beta_1^2 S_{xx}\) terms cancel, leaving:

\[= (n-1)\sigma^2 - \sigma^2 = (n-2)\sigma^2\]

\[\mathbb{E}(\text{MSE}) = \mathbb{E}\!\left(\frac{\text{RSS}}{n-2}\right) = \frac{(n-2)\sigma^2}{n-2} = \sigma^2 \qquad \]

The Residual Standard Deviation

Taking the square root gives the residual standard deviation:

\[\widehat{\sigma} = \sqrt{\widehat{\sigma}^2} = \sqrt{\frac{\text{RSS}}{n-2}}\]

Interpretation: \(\widehat{\sigma}\) is the typical size of a prediction error, on the scale of \(y\).

Tip

Compare \(\widehat{\sigma}\) to the spread of \(y\) to gauge model usefulness. A small \(\widehat{\sigma}\) relative to the range of \(y\) indicates a good fit.

Standard Errors of the Estimators

Since \(\sigma^2\) is unknown, we replace it with \(\widehat{\sigma}^2\) in the variance formulas:

\[\widehat{\text{Var}}(\widehat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\widehat{\sigma}^2}{S_{xx}}, \qquad \widehat{\text{Var}}(\widehat{\beta}_0) = \widehat{\sigma}^2\!\left(\frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{xx}}\right)\]

The standard errors are the square roots:

\[\text{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\widehat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}, \qquad \text{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_0) = \widehat{\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{xx}}}\]

Standard errors quantify the uncertainty in each estimate — they are the basis for confidence intervals and hypothesis tests (coming soon).

Fitting Linear Regression in R

The lm() Function

In R, we fit a linear regression model using lm():

lm(y ~ x, data = your_data)
  • y is the response variable
  • x is the predictor variable
  • The ~ reads as “is modeled as a function of”
  • The result is an object we can pass to summary(), fitted(), residuals(), etc.

Example: Air Quality in New York

The built-in airquality dataset records daily air quality measurements in New York (May–September 1973).

  • X: Temperature (°F)
  • Y: Ozone concentration (ppb)
aq <- na.omit(airquality[, c("Temp", "Ozone")])

plot(aq$Temp, aq$Ozone,
     xlab = "Temperature (°F)",
     ylab = "Ozone (ppb)")

Fitting the Model

fit <- lm(Ozone ~ Temp, data = aq)
summary(fit)

Interpreting the Output

From the output above, we read off the key values directly:

Air Quality Example

We have \(\widehat{\beta}_0 = -147.65\), \(\widehat{\beta}_1 = 2.44\), and \(\widehat{\sigma} = 23.71\). Thus \(\widehat{\sigma}^2 = 23.71^2 \approx 562.2\).

Fitted model: \(\widehat{\text{Ozone}} = -147.65 + 2.44 \times \text{Temp}\)

The intercept is included by default. Use formula shortcuts to change this:

Formula Model
lm(y ~ x) \(Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_i + \varepsilon_i\) — intercept included
lm(y ~ x - 1) \(Y_i = \beta_1 X_i + \varepsilon_i\) — no intercept (line through origin)
lm(y ~ 1) \(Y_i = \beta_0 + \varepsilon_i\) — intercept only (estimates the mean of \(Y\))

Tip

sigma() returns \(\widehat{\sigma} = \sqrt{\text{RSS}/(n-2)}\) — the same quantity we computed by hand today.

Summary

  • Residuals \(e_i = y_i - \widehat{y}_i\) are observed proxies for the unobservable errors \(\varepsilon_i\)
  • Five properties of OLS residuals follow from the normal equations: \(\sum e_i = 0\), \(\sum \widehat{y}_i = \sum y_i\), \(\sum x_i e_i = 0\), \(\sum \widehat{y}_i e_i = 0\), and the line passes through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\)
  • RSS \(= \sum e_i^2\) measures total unexplained variation — OLS minimizes it by construction
  • \(\widehat{\sigma}^2 = \text{RSS}/(n-2)\) is an unbiased estimate of \(\sigma^2\); dividing by \(n-2\) corrects for two constraints placed on the residuals
  • \(\widehat{\sigma}\) is the residual standard deviation — the typical prediction error on the scale of \(y\)
  • Standard errors replace \(\sigma\) with \(\widehat{\sigma}\) in the variance formulas — the foundation for inference
  • Use lm() in R to fit the model and summary(), residuals(), sigma() to extract results
