Review: The Simple Linear Model

From Monday, the simple linear regression model is:

\[y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i, \quad \varepsilon_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\]

  • \(\beta_1\) is a comparison between units — not a causal effect
  • The fitted line \(\hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x\) summarizes the relationship

Today: How do we estimate \(\hat{\beta}_0\), \(\hat{\beta}_1\), and \(\sigma\) from data?

Learning Objectives

By the end of today you will be able to:

  • Explain the least squares criterion and apply it conceptually
  • State the OLS formulas for the slope and intercept
  • Compute and interpret fitted values and residuals
  • Interpret the residual standard deviation \(\hat{\sigma}\) and \(R^2\)
  • Explain what a standard error measures
  • Use lm() in R to fit a simple linear regression and read its output

How Do We Fit a Line?

The Problem of Fitting

Given data \((x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \ldots, (x_n, y_n)\), infinitely many lines pass through or near the points.

We need a criterion to select the “best” line.

Intuition: The best line should make prediction errors as small as possible across all observations simultaneously.

Residuals: Measuring Prediction Error

For a candidate line with intercept \(a\) and slope \(b\), the residual for observation \(i\) is:

\[e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i = y_i - (a + b x_i)\]

  • \(\hat{y}_i = a + bx_i\): the fitted value (what the line predicts)
  • \(e_i > 0\): observed point is above the line
  • \(e_i < 0\): observed point is below the line

A good line has small residuals throughout.

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)

The Least Squares Criterion

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) chooses \(\hat{\beta}_0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_1\) to minimize the Residual Sum of Squares (RSS):

\[\text{RSS}(a, b) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - a - b x_i)^2\]

Why square the residuals?

  • Positive and negative errors don’t cancel each other out
  • Large errors are penalized more heavily than small ones
  • The squared criterion has a clean, closed-form solution

Deriving the OLS Estimators

Minimize RSS by taking partial derivatives with respect to \(a\) and \(b\), setting them to zero, and solving:

\[\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}\]

\[\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}\]

Note

The OLS line always passes through the point \((\bar{x},\, \bar{y})\).

A Useful Rewrite

The OLS slope can also be written as:

\[\hat{\beta}_1 = r \cdot \frac{s_y}{s_x}\]

where \(r\) is the sample correlation between \(x\) and \(y\), and \(s_x\), \(s_y\) are sample standard deviations.

  • When \(r = 0\): slope is 0 — no linear relationship
  • When \(|r| = 1\): all points lie exactly on a line
  • The sign of \(\hat{\beta}_1\) always equals the sign of \(r\)

Residuals and Model Fit

Accessing Residuals in R

residuals(fit)   # e_i = y_i - y_hat_i
fitted(fit)      # y_hat_i = beta0_hat + beta1_hat * x_i

# Residual plot
plot(fitted(fit), residuals(fit),
     xlab = "Fitted values",
     ylab = "Residuals",
     main = "Residuals vs. Fitted")
abline(h = 0, lty = 2, col = "gray50")

A well-fitted model has residuals that are centered at zero with no systematic pattern.

Estimating \(\sigma\)

The true error variance \(\sigma^2\) is unknown — we estimate it from the residuals:

\[\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{\text{RSS}}{n - 2} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{n - 2}\]

We divide by \(n - 2\) — not \(n\) — because we estimated two parameters (\(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\)) from the data, using up 2 degrees of freedom.

What Does \(\hat{\sigma}\) Tell Us?

\(\hat{\sigma}\) is the residual standard deviation — the typical size of prediction errors, on the scale of \(y\).

For the election model: \(\hat{\sigma} = 3.76\) percentage points.

Interpretation: Even with the best linear prediction using income growth, vote shares typically deviate from predictions by about 3.8 percentage points.

Tip

Compare \(\hat{\sigma}\) to the spread of \(y\) to assess predictive accuracy. A small \(\hat{\sigma}\) relative to the range of \(y\) indicates a useful model.

\(R^2\): Proportion of Variance Explained

\[R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{RSS}}{\text{TSS}} = 1 - \frac{\displaystyle\sum(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\displaystyle\sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2}\]

  • TSS = Total Sum of Squares — variance in \(y\) if we ignored \(x\) entirely
  • \(R^2 = 0\): the model explains nothing; predicting \(\bar{y}\) is just as good
  • \(R^2 = 1\): the model explains everything; residuals are all zero
  • Election model: \(R^2 = 0.57\) — income growth explains 57% of the variation in vote share

Caution

High \(R^2\) does not mean the model is correct or causal — it only measures linear fit.

Standard Errors

What is a Standard Error?

A standard error measures the precision of a sample estimate.

Imagine drawing many independent samples of size \(n\) from the same population and computing \(\hat{\beta}_1\) each time. The standard deviation of those estimates across samples is the standard error of \(\hat{\beta}_1\).

\[\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}} = \frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}}\]

What Makes Standard Errors Smaller?

\[\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2}}\]

Standard errors are smaller (more precise estimates) when:

  • \(\hat{\sigma}\) is small — less noise in the data
  • Predictors \(x\) are more spread out — more information about the slope
  • Sample size \(n\) is larger — more data overall

Confidence Intervals for Coefficients

A 95% confidence interval for \(\beta_1\) is:

\[\hat{\beta}_1 \pm t_{n-2,\,0.025} \cdot \text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1)\]

For the election model (\(n = 16\), \(t_{14, 0.025} \approx 2.14\)):

\[3.06 \pm 2.14 \times 0.71 \approx (1.54,\; 4.58)\]

Interpretation: We are 95% confident the true slope lies between 1.54 and 4.58 — a 1% increase in income growth is associated with between 1.5 and 4.6 more percentage points of vote share.

Regression as Comparison

Comparing Two Groups as Regression

Regression is a generalization of simpler statistical comparisons.

Comparing a single mean: regress \(y\) on just a constant: \[y_i = \mu + \varepsilon_i \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \hat{\mu} = \bar{y}\]

Comparing two groups: let \(x_i \in \{0, 1\}\) be a binary group indicator: \[y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i\]

  • \(\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y}_{\text{group 0}}\)
  • \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \bar{y}_{\text{group 1}} - \bar{y}_{\text{group 0}}\)

This is algebraically equivalent to a two-sample \(t\)-test (assuming equal variances).

Regression Generalizes the \(t\)-test

# Suppose x is a binary 0/1 indicator (e.g., treatment vs. control)
t.test(y ~ x, var.equal = TRUE)

# Produces the same estimate as:
lm(y ~ x)

Regression is strictly more general — it can:

  • Handle continuous predictors
  • Adjust for additional variables simultaneously (multiple regression)
  • Accommodate more than two groups via indicator coding

Making Predictions

Given a new observation with predictor value \(x^*\), the predicted outcome is:

\[\hat{y}^* = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x^*\]

Example: What vote share does the model predict when income growth = 2%?

\[\hat{y} = 46.2 + 3.1 \times 2 = 52.4\%\]

Extrapolation

Predicting far outside the range of the observed \(x\) values is unreliable. The linear relationship may not hold beyond the data.

Brief Intro: Fake-Data Simulation

A powerful way to validate our fitting procedure: simulate data from a known model, then check whether we can recover the true parameters.

set.seed(42)
n     <- 50
x     <- runif(n, min = 0, max = 10)

# True (known) parameters
beta0 <- 2
beta1 <- 3
sigma <- 5

y   <- beta0 + beta1 * x + rnorm(n, mean = 0, sd = sigma)
fit <- lm(y ~ x)
coef(fit)  # should be close to (2, 3)

If estimates are far from the truth on simulated data, something is wrong with our procedure.

Coefficients as Comparisons

Regression Coefficients Describe Comparisons

A regression slope \(\hat{\beta}_1\) compares groups of units that differ by 1 unit in \(x\).

\(\hat{\beta}_1\) is the estimated average difference in \(y\) between two otherwise-similar observations where \(x\) differs by 1 unit.

Correlation \(\neq\) Causation

Regression coefficients describe associations in data. They do not, by themselves, establish causal relationships.

Why “Effect” Language Can Mislead

Consider: “Each year of education increases income by $5,000.”

This phrasing implies a causal mechanism — that giving someone more education would change their income by $5,000.

What the regression actually estimates:

“People with one more year of education earn, on average, $5,000 more than people with one fewer year of education.”

The difference matters when drawing policy conclusions.

A Better Framing

Avoid:

“Education increases income by $5,000.”

Prefer:

“Comparing people who differ by one year of education, those with more education earn $5,000 more on average.”

Regression describes comparisons across units in the data. Causal claims require additional study design considerations beyond this course.

Summary

  • OLS chooses \(\hat{\beta}_0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_1\) by minimizing \(\text{RSS} = \sum(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\)
  • Closed-form solution: \(\hat{\beta}_1 = S_{xy}/S_{xx}\), \(\;\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}\)
  • Residuals \(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\) measure prediction errors
  • \(\hat{\sigma}\) is the residual SD — typical prediction error — estimated by \(\sqrt{\text{RSS}/(n-2)}\)
  • \(R^2\) is the proportion of variance in \(y\) explained by \(x\) — useful but limited
  • Standard errors quantify uncertainty in coefficient estimates
  • Regression with a binary predictor generalizes the two-sample \(t\)-test
  • Fake-data simulation is a principled tool for validating our fitting procedure
